# Rising Ashes — War Command, Tactical Map and Military Rulebook
1. The core war system
Every battle happens somewhere real.
If two armies meet beside a river, the tactical map is generated from that actual part of the world.
If you attack a mountain fortress, the map contains its real:
walls
roads
gates
cliffs
bridges
forests
villages
farmland
rivers
elevation
watchtowers
supply roads
You are therefore not selecting:
Battlefield #4
You are fighting for an actual location that exists in the world.
The tactical map is effectively a simplified military representation of that real place.
2. Three command views
The player can change how the war map is displayed without changing the simulation underneath it.
Realistic map
Looks like a detailed topographic military map.
Muted terrain.
Rivers.
Roads.
Elevation.
Settlements.
Small military symbols.
Very little bright coloring.
Best for immersion.
Tactical 2D
Cleaner.
Dark terrain.
White or beige roads.
Army formations represented through clear shapes.
Movement lines.
Attack arrows.
Visibility cones.
Supply routes.
Best for serious command.
War-table mode
Looks like you physically entered your War Room.
The map is sitting on a table.
Wooden or metal army pieces represent forces.
Officers surround you.
Messengers arrive.
You can zoom into the table.
This should become the most immersive option.
All three display the same war.
3. Armies are not one marker
An army of 2,000 soldiers shouldn't be one dot.
It has internal formations.
For example:
Army of 2,040
General Varos
First Infantry Battalion — 400
Second Infantry Battalion — 400
Spear Battalion — 300
Archers — 240
Heavy Cavalry — 180
Light Cavalry — 220
Mage Company — 80
Engineers — 70
Scouts — 60
Medical/Supply — 90
Specialists — 60
Every major formation can move separately.
4. Splitting formations
You can select:
2nd Infantry — 400
Then split:
2nd Infantry A — 250
2nd Infantry B — 150
Now you can send 150 troops to protect a bridge while 250 continue forward.
Later you can merge them again.
This happens throughout battle.
You can also detach:
20 cavalry
40 archers
one mage squad
one scout team
from larger formations.
The game shouldn't force perfectly round military units.
War becomes messy.
5. Dragging units during battle
This is one of the main interfaces.
Select a formation.
Drag its destination.
Then optionally assign behavior.
Examples:
Advance.
Hold.
Charge.
Harass.
Flank.
Screen.
Escort.
Ambush.
Retreat.
Withdraw fighting.
Capture.
Defend.
Intercept.
Follow another formation.
Attack only if attacked.
Avoid engagement.
The unit begins executing the instruction.
It doesn't teleport.
6. Movement occurs visibly
You should watch your pieces physically moving.
For example:

```
          ENEMY MAIN FORCE
                 ●●●
                  ↓

FOREST        River Crossing
██████             ║
██████             ║
                   ║

      Cavalry → →  ║
                   ║
       ●●●● Infantry
             ↑
           YOU
```

The markers gradually move.
Scouts report enemies.
Lines change.
Battles appear.
Units retreat.
Reserves move forward.
This continues even while you're examining another part of the battlefield.
7. Contact becomes battle
Two opposing formations reaching each other creates an engagement.
You might see:
North Ridge Engagement
Your Forces:
312 infantry
58 archers
Enemy estimate:
250–400 infantry
unknown support
Commander:
Captain Seran
Status:
Holding.
You can leave Captain Seran in command or begin issuing more detailed instructions.
8. It is NOT random auto-resolve
Battle calculations should come from understandable systems.
The major factors are:
troop quality
commander ability
formation
terrain
morale
fatigue
equipment
elemental capability
intelligence
numbers
position
supplies
weather
experience
communication
surprise
Random variation exists only for believable uncertainty.
A brilliant commander with veteran troops doesn't suddenly lose to peasants because the game rolled 7 instead of 9.

Eventually one of the most important things in Rising Ashes becomes finding talented people.
A general has attributes such as:

But personality matters too.
10. Commander personalities
Two generals with identical tactical skill can behave differently.
An aggressive commander might exploit openings instantly.
A cautious commander may wait for confirmation.
A stubborn commander might refuse withdrawal until late.
An inventive commander may improvise.
A loyal commander may follow terrible orders.
An independent commander might ignore orders if they believe they're suicidal.
That means recruiting commanders is about more than stats.
11. Command capacity
A person can't effectively control infinite troops.
Every commander has a practical command capacity.
Example:
Poor officer:
50–100 troops.
Capable captain:
200–400.
Experienced commander:
1,000–2,000.
Great general:
several thousand.
Exceptional strategic commander:
entire armies.
Trying to give 3,000 troops to an inexperienced officer creates:
slow orders
confusion
formation breakdown
lower morale
delayed responses
12. Military rank controls your authority
You don't automatically gain control of an army because you're the player.
If you join the Valencios military as an ordinary soldier, you control:
your own character.
Nothing else.
Later:
Squad Leader
perhaps 5–12 soldiers.
Sergeant
perhaps 20–40.
Lieutenant
perhaps 60–150.
Captain
perhaps 200–500.
Commander
multiple formations.
General
an army.
Campaign Marshal
multiple armies.
Your exact authority depends on the military structure of that kingdom.
13. You can be denied orders
If you are only a lieutenant, you cannot drag another captain's troops across the battlefield.
They don't belong to your command.
You could request assistance.
Your superior decides.
Authority becomes part of gameplay.
14. Appointment to campaign command
Sometimes a kingdom may appoint you to command a specific war.
Now your permissions temporarily expand.
The king might give you command of:
three armies
four fortresses
regional militias
supply networks
After the war, those forces are not automatically yours.
They belong to the kingdom.
15. Personal armies
If you own a settlement, mercenary company, guild, sect or noble estate, your own troops work differently.
They answer to you.
You decide:
recruitment
training
equipment
commanders
formations
deployment
This becomes the foundation of your independent military power.
16. Battlefield command hierarchy
Large armies need sub-commanders.
You might issue:
General Varos
Take the northern wing.
Under him:
Captain Arlen — infantry.
Captain Mei — archers.
Commander Joro — cavalry.
You can order General Varos:
“Capture North Hill.”
Varos decides exactly how his subordinate units attempt it.
Or you can take direct control and command the individual formations yourself.
17. Micromanagement has consequences
Controlling everything personally shouldn't always be superior.
When commanding 15 formations simultaneously, you may miss something.
Using competent officers reduces your workload.
Weak officers require supervision.
Great generals can independently win sections of the battle.
This makes recruitment meaningful.
18. Battlefield assistants
Eventually you can appoint:
chief strategist
military secretary
scout master
logistics officer
siege engineer
intelligence officer
Their purpose isn't simply +10 stats.
They actively assist you.
Your strategist might say:
Enemy cavalry has disappeared behind the eastern ridge. They may be preparing to flank us.
Your logistics officer:
We can hold this position for roughly three more days before grain becomes critical.
Your siege engineer:
Their eastern wall is older. Concentrated bombardment there should create a breach faster.
You decide whether to listen.
19. Bad advisors exist
An advisor may:
misread information
be inexperienced
have outdated doctrine
panic
lie
have political motives
Therefore:
high Intelligence ≠ automatically correct.
Their knowledge and experience matter.
20. Historical-style battlefield signaling
The system can draw inspiration from historical East Asian and medieval military command without copying one single historical army exactly.
Commands can travel through:
banners
drums
horns
flags
runners
mounted messengers
signal fires
magical signals
Different cultures develop different command traditions.
This also creates actual gameplay.
21. Communication range
Nearby formations can receive commands quickly.
Far formations may require a messenger.
Example:
You change the left wing's orders.
Distance:
1.6 km.
Messenger travel:
approximately 4 minutes.
For four minutes, that commander continues executing the previous plan.
22. Losing communication
A messenger can:
get killed
get lost
be captured
arrive late
Enemy forces can deliberately disrupt communication.
Therefore battlefield chaos can develop naturally.
23. Fog of war
You only know what your forces can observe.
A forest might show:
Enemy Force
Estimated:
200–600.
You don't know exactly.
Send scouts.
Later:
Confirmed:
417 infantry.
32 mounted soldiers.
Possible mage support.
This dramatically changes strategy.
24. Information can become outdated
A scout reports:
Enemy army at Redhill.
Thirty minutes later you still see the marker there.
But they may already have moved.
The map displays:
Last confirmed:
32 minutes ago.
This prevents the map from becoming magical omniscience.
25. Formation system
You can arrange units before battle.
Examples:
Line.
Deep line.
Column.
Wedge.
Crescent.
Encirclement.
Shield formation.
Spear wall.
Loose formation.
Defensive square.
Layered defense.
Reserve line.
But formations aren't magical buffs.
They have actual advantages and disadvantages.
26. Formation depth
A real formation isn't simply:
Infantry.
You can create:
Front:
Heavy shields.
Second line:
Spearmen.
Third:
Archers.
Rear:
Mages.
Flanks:
Cavalry.
Reserve:
Veteran infantry.
The map visually represents these layers.
27. Reserve forces
Good commanders don't throw everything into battle immediately.
You can keep troops in reserve.
When the enemy commits cavalry:
deploy reserve spearmen.
When an enemy flank collapses:
send reserve cavalry through the opening.
When your center breaks:
use veterans to stabilize it.
This creates genuine tactical decisions.
28. Terrain determines tactics
Terrain must matter enormously.
Forest:
slower formations
cavalry weakened
ambush opportunities
visibility reduced
Open plains:
cavalry powerful
long sightlines
ranged troops effective
Mountains:
narrow routes
high-ground advantage
fatigue
ambushes
Swamps:
slow movement
disease risk
heavy armor problems
Cities:
streets
rooftops
chokepoints
barricades
No universal “best army” exists.
29. Outmaneuvering the AI
The tactical AI should obey the same rules as the player.
It scouts.
It evaluates terrain.
It protects supplies.
It hides forces.
It uses reserves.
It attempts flanks.
It retreats.
It adapts.
Stronger commanders use more advanced behaviors.
Therefore difficulty isn't created by:
Enemy +200% damage.
It's created by:
better decisions.
30. AI commander tiers
Weak commander:
reactive.
Leaves obvious openings.
Uses simple frontal attacks.
Moderate commander:
uses reserves.
Protects flanks.
Responds to terrain.
Advanced commander:
creates feints.
Hides movement.
Attacks logistics.
Uses multiple battle phases.
Elite commander:
anticipates your strategy.
Manipulates information.
Uses sacrificial positions.
Coordinates multiple armies.
Changes plans mid-battle.
Legendary commander:
can completely reshape a campaign through planning.
31. Tactical doctrine
Different nations shouldn't use the same AI.
Valencios might favor:
disciplined infantry
fortifications
combined arms
Eastern armies might favor:
large coordinated formations
signals
layered reserves
battlefield control
Steppe cultures:
mobility
horse archery
false retreats
Forest cultures:
ambush
small formations
terrain manipulation
Military culture becomes visible through how armies behave.
32. False retreat
One advanced tactic:
send cavalry forward.
Engage briefly.
Retreat.
Enemy pursues.
Hidden archers and infantry attack their exposed formation.
This only works if:
enemy commander takes the bait.
Experienced generals may recognize it.
33. Feints
Order 300 infantry to appear ready for an attack.
Enemy reinforces that side.
Your actual main force attacks elsewhere.
Scouting and commander intelligence determine whether deception works.
34. Encirclement
If you successfully surround an enemy:
morale drops.
retreat becomes harder.
supply access disappears.
But encirclement requires enough troops and terrain control.
A desperate enemy may attempt to break through one section.
35. Defeat in detail
If two enemy armies are separated:
attack the smaller one before reinforcement arrives.
Messenger information, scouting and travel speed determine whether you can execute this.
36. Night operations
You can attempt:
night marches
night raids
camp infiltration
bridge destruction
supply attacks
Risks increase:
units get lost
friendly confusion
ambushes
command becomes harder
Experienced troops perform better.
37. Weather warfare
Heavy rain can:
reduce archery
create mud
slow cavalry
raise rivers
Fog:
low visibility.
Snow:
movement exhaustion.
Extreme heat:
stamina loss.
Commanders experienced in the region can exploit this.
38. Elemental warfare
Rising Ashes warfare should eventually become much deeper because of the power systems.
Earth users could:
raise barriers
collapse terrain
fortify positions
Wind users:
disrupt arrows
improve movement
spread smoke
Fire users:
burn defenses
deny terrain
Water users:
manipulate rivers
create slippery terrain
counter fire
But powerful magic is limited.
It consumes energy.
And countermeasures exist.
39. Elite individuals
A powerful individual can affect battles.
But not automatically defeat thousands.
A master bender might destroy one formation.
A legendary knight may break a defensive line.
A powerful mage might breach a gate.
This creates strategic targets.
Kill or neutralize the enemy's elite combatants.
40. Duels between champions
Sometimes two exceptional fighters may meet during battle.
You can:

send your champion
avoid them
focus ranged attacks
isolate them
Different cultures may even formally recognize battlefield duels.

siege workshops

44. Siege engines

battering ram

45. Breaches
A wall breach creates a new battlefield point.
You can drag units toward it.
But defenders know that too.
They may create a second defensive line behind the breach.
So destroying a wall doesn't automatically win.
46. Street warfare
Capturing the wall may lead to fighting through:
streets
courtyards
markets
bridges
inner walls
palaces
Cities can have several defensive layers.
47. Surrender
Enemies evaluate whether continuing makes sense.
Factors include:
food
morale
reinforcements
commander personality
civilian situation
politics
your reputation
A commander who believes you massacre prisoners may refuse surrender even when defeat is certain.
Your past actions therefore affect future wars.
48. Real-time battle entry
At any moment during many battles you may choose:
Join Battle Personally
You enter the actual battlefield.
The strategic situation remains identical.
The units you positioned on the map appear exactly there.
You can now fight with them.
49. Live battlefield commands
While fighting personally, you can still command forces.
Quick commands might include:

Hold.
Advance.

50. Returning to command map
You can leave personal combat command and return to the strategic view.
You aren't changing game modes.
You're simply changing perspective.
This is essential.
51. Campaign warfare
Above individual battles exists the larger campaign map.
You might simultaneously have:
Army A defending your settlement.
Army B moving toward enemy territory.
Army C protecting supplies.
Scouts searching mountains.
A siege occurring east.
Bandits raiding your farms.
You decide where to focus.
52. Campaign logistics
Distance becomes extremely important.
An army 400 kilometres away isn't something you casually move across the map.
It requires:
days or weeks
supplies
roads
rest
money
Campaign planning therefore matters before war begins.
53. Military intelligence
Before declaring war you might spend months gathering:
fortress maps
commander information
troop estimates
supply information
alliance information
roads
secret entrances
A prepared war is completely different from an impulsive one.
54. War planning room
Before major wars, you can call a council.
Your officers gather.
The map appears.
You propose targets.
Generals give recommendations.
Example:
Strategist:
“Attacking Redwall directly will cost thousands. Capture its northern farms first and the garrison will lose food.”
Cavalry commander:
“The northern fields are open. Give me three hundred riders.”
Siege engineer:
“Give me twelve days and I can construct heavy engines.”
You decide.

59. Claim system
Land remembers previous ownership.
If you conquer a fortress from Valencios:
Valencios receives a claim.
Their nobles may demand its return twenty years later.
Another kingdom may also possess an older claim.
This generates future conflicts naturally.
60. Tactical rulebook
The game should internally teach real tactical principles.
Players eventually learn:
Never expose supply lines.
Don't commit reserves too early.
High ground matters.
Information wins battles.
Don't pursue blindly.
Protect your commander.
Cavalry needs space.
Archers need sightlines.
Heavy troops tire.
A retreat route is essential.
Scouting before movement prevents disaster.
Large forces aren't always stronger in narrow terrain.
Sieges consume enormous resources.
Weather can invalidate a plan.
A good general is worth more than hundreds of additional inexperienced soldiers.
These shouldn't merely be loading-screen tips.
The simulation should actually make them true.
61. Why talented NPCs eventually become priceless
Early game:
you care mostly about your own strength.
Mid game:
you need companions.
Later:
you realize you cannot personally:
command three armies
govern six settlements
run logistics
lead scouts
manage diplomacy
fight personally
Then NPC talent becomes one of the most valuable resources in the world.
That childhood academy rival with exceptional strategic ability?
You may desperately want them as your general twenty years later.
The former classmate who became a logistics genius?
They can keep your 10,000-person army alive.
The person you ignored early in the story may now control a fortress you need.
This connects the life simulation directly to warfare.
62. The intended feeling
At first:
You are one poor child.
Then:
You learn to fight.
Then:
You control yourself.
Then:
A few companions.
Then:
A squad.
Then:
A company.
Then:
Several formations.
Eventually you're standing over a campaign map while:
three armies march
messengers arrive
a fortress is starving
your cavalry disappeared behind enemy territory
an allied lord requests reinforcement
your strategist believes the enemy is preparing a false retreat
your home settlement needs troops
and an enemy army has unexpectedly crossed the southern river.
And you actually have to decide what to do.
That is the point where the player's progression from a poor commoner to a real military power becomes tangible.
Fundamental rule
Rising Ashes warfare should reward understanding, preparation and people—not merely higher numbers.
An inexperienced player commanding 8,000 troops should be capable of losing to a brilliant commander with 5,000.
And when the player eventually wins difficult campaigns, it should feel like they won because they actually understood the battlefield.

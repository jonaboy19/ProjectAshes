# Rising Ashes — Realm, War and Settlement Simulation

## Core principle

The player should begin as a person living inside the world, not as a commander selecting armies from a menu.

Everything above the player must be earned.

A character might begin with:

no land
almost no money
one weapon
a rented room or family home
no soldiers
no political influence
little knowledge of the world
no authority to issue military orders

The same character could eventually possess:

an estate
a trading company
a personal retinue
a fortified settlement
several villages
roads and watchtowers
tamed Soulbeasts
hundreds or thousands of troops
vassals
alliances
political marriages
a noble title
a regional military command
and eventually a crown

The progression between those states should be the game.

---

# 1. Followers are real people

Recruitment should not be a generic “+1 soldier” system.

Every recruited character remains an individual NPC.

A follower can have:

name
age
origin
family
occupation
combat ability
elemental affinity
personality
religion
loyalty
morale
relationships
ambitions
salary expectations
equipment
injuries
skills
fears
preferred fighting style
friends and enemies
current physical location

That means recruiting a hunter is different from recruiting a trained cavalryman.

A hunter might be:

excellent at tracking
cheap to maintain
knowledgeable about forests
poor in formal battles
capable of teaching archery

A knight might be:

extremely expensive
politically connected
well equipped
difficult to control
valuable for training soldiers
capable of attracting additional retainers

People should not simply disappear into an inventory.

They physically live somewhere.

---

# 2. Calling your people

This is one of the systems that could make Rising Ashes distinctive.

You do not instantly teleport your followers to yourself.

You issue a summons.

Methods can evolve with progression:

messenger
carrier bird
merchant courier
signal fire
military runner
horn network
church courier
rune beacon
magical communication artifact

If your cavalry captain is 60 kilometres away, he might need two days to arrive.

If your scout happens to be in the nearby village, she could arrive within an hour.

A follower can also respond:

“I will come.”

“I cannot leave my post.”

“We are trapped by flooding.”

“The eastern road is unsafe.”

“My unit lost three men crossing the pass.”

“The messenger never arrived.”

This immediately creates stories without scripted quests.

---

# 3. Camps

Your first settlement should usually be a camp rather than a village.

A camp is dangerous.

The player selects actual terrain.

Forest clearing.

River bank.

Hilltop.

Road junction.

Abandoned ruins.

Rift frontier.

Mountain pass.

The land itself affects the settlement.

A river gives water and fishing.

A hill improves defense.

A forest gives wood but attracts creatures.

A road improves trade but increases visibility.

A Rift frontier gives valuable resources but enormous danger.

# 5. Roads matter

Roads should actually affect simulation.

A good road increases:

travel speed
merchant traffic
army movement
messenger speed

construction transport

But roads can also help enemies reach you.

Players could build:

dirt paths

# 6. Settlement progression

Do not make progression simply:

Camp → Village → Town → City.

Instead, settlements evolve according to what the player actually creates.

A camp can become a:

merchant settlement
military fortress
religious commune
mining colony
farming village
monster-tamer enclave
frontier city
naval port
scholar city
Rift expedition base

The game should recognize its identity.

Population and buildings determine classification.

For example:

20 residents + basic huts = camp

150 residents + farms + market = village

800 residents + defenses + trades = town

large walls + military infrastructure = stronghold

large population + advanced economy = city

political authority + surrounding territory = regional capital

But there should be exceptions.

A fortress with only 400 residents may be militarily more important than a town of 3,000.

There can be:

royal land

village commons

abandoned land
Rift territory

Possible ways to gain land include:

purchase

Land deeds matter.

A noble may later claim:

Now you have a legal conflict.

---

# 8. Conquest does not equal ownership

This is essential.

Capturing a settlement only means:

you currently control it.

It does not mean everyone accepts you.

After conquest you may face:

rebellion
sabotage
assassination attempts
economic collapse
refugees
loyalist militias
religious resistance
former rulers
foreign intervention
bandit activity
food shortages
enemy counterattacks

A settlement's population has its own loyalty.

You may physically own the fortress while the surrounding population hates you.

---

# 9. War room

Once your settlement becomes advanced enough, you can construct a **War Room**.

This unlocks strategic command.

The War Room contains a physical campaign map.

The character walks into the room.

Officers stand around the table.

Messengers arrive.

Scouts update information.

Markers represent forces.

This is not simply a pause menu.

It exists inside the world.

---

# 10. The campaign map

The map only displays information your faction actually knows.

If you have never explored a valley, it might be blank.

If a scout visited six months ago, the information can be outdated.

An army marker doesn't necessarily mean:

“342 enemies are here.”

Instead it may say:

Unknown force
Estimated 200–500
Possibly cavalry
Last seen two days ago

Better reconnaissance produces better intelligence.

---

# 11. Strategic army movement

You select forces and issue orders.

Example:

3rd Infantry Company
120 troops

Travel:

Redwater Camp → Greybridge → Eastern Pass

Orders:

Avoid major engagements.

Protect merchants.

Establish camp at Eastern Pass.

Estimated arrival:

2 days, 7 hours.

But the army actually travels through the world simulation.

Weather can slow it.

Enemies can intercept it.

Food can run out.

A bridge may be destroyed.

A monster migration can block the road.

---

# 12. Orders have communication delay

This makes warfare dramatically more interesting.

Suppose your army is three days away.

You order it to retreat.

A courier must physically travel there.

Your troops continue following their old orders until the message arrives.

You might later receive:

“Message received two days ago. General Varin already engaged the enemy.”

Now you must respond to what happened rather than controlling everything instantly.

---

# 13. Commander autonomy

Generals should have personality.

You can give someone orders like:

Hold this fortress.

But how they accomplish that depends on them.

An aggressive commander may sortie.

A cautious commander may remain behind walls.

A corrupt commander might steal supplies.

A brilliant commander might exploit terrain.

A coward may retreat.

A loyal commander may die defending your position.

Choosing who commands matters almost as much as troop strength.

---

# 14. Live war

You can personally join battles.

Then the game transitions naturally into normal gameplay.

You ride with your soldiers.

You command nearby formations.

You fight.

You use elemental abilities.

You protect officers.

You retreat physically.

You siege walls.

You fight enemy champions.

The strategic simulation continues around you.

---

# 15. Strategic-resolution war

You shouldn't have to personally fight every battle.

You can remain at headquarters and command through the map.

Battles resolve through:

army composition
terrain
leadership
morale
supplies
fortifications
elemental abilities
weather
intelligence
fatigue
strategy

But avoid simplistic:

Army Power 7,000 > Army Power 6,000.

A smaller force can win.

---

# 16. Hybrid warfare

This is where the system becomes especially strong.

Imagine three battles occurring simultaneously.

You personally fight at the central fortress.

Your northern general fights autonomously.

Your southern troops are controlled through strategic commands.

All three are part of one war.

# 21. Warfare without a crest

You can conduct covert operations.

Wear neutral equipment.

Operate as supposed bandits.

Possible missions:

raid caravans
burn supplies
destroy bridges
kidnap officers
attack patrols

Witnesses.

Known magical techniques.

Someone might discover:

“These weren't bandits. They were Valencian soldiers.”

Now you have an international incident.

# 22. Reputation of warfare

Your military behavior creates reputation.

A ruler who consistently:

protects civilians
honors surrender
pays soldiers
keeps agreements

may gain a reputation for honor.

Another may become feared because prisoners disappear.

NPCs react accordingly.

Some troops may refuse immoral commands.

Others may specifically join brutal leaders.

# 31. Creature taming

Creatures should not become pets because you reduced their HP.

Taming methods vary by species.

A wolf-like beast may require trust.

A magical stag may require elemental resonance.

A wyvern may require raising it from youth.

A territorial beast may accept dominance.

Some creatures can never truly be domesticated.

# 33. Regional difficulty

Every region should display something like:

Recommended preparation.

Not just “Level 30.”

It might say:

Moderate monster activity

Church patrols active

# 34. Territory memory

One particularly strong system:

**The world remembers ownership.**

If your family controlled a fortress 40 years ago, descendants may still consider it theirs.

Villagers may tell stories.

Old banners remain hidden.

Political claims can survive generations.

Territory therefore has history rather than simply changing map colour.

---

# 35. War councils

Before major campaigns, your officers can gather.

They offer plans.

One wants a frontal assault.

Another proposes starving the fortress.

A scout discovers a mountain path.

A mage suggests destroying the bridge.

You choose.

Their advice depends on personality and competence.

They can also be wrong.

# 38. Generational consequences

Eventually the system can support families.

NPCs age.

Children grow.

Students become masters.

Soldiers become commanders.

Villagers remember wars.

A recruit you hired at 19 could become your general at 42.

This is how Rising Ashes can create stories the developers never manually wrote.

---

# 39. The player should never control everything

This is important.

Large-scale power should introduce uncertainty.

When you have five followers, you know everything.

When you command 15,000 people, you don't.

Information becomes incomplete.

Delegation becomes necessary.

Corruption becomes possible.

Politics becomes harder.

That makes becoming powerful interesting instead of simply making the game easier.

---

Even if you're king, there are still:

Being king simply opens another scale of gameplay.

The progression becomes:

Individual

→ Camp

# The important design rule

Rising Ashes shouldn't have separate “systems” sitting beside each other.

The settlement system feeds the economy.

The economy feeds recruitment.

Recruitment feeds warfare.

Warfare changes diplomacy.

Diplomacy changes trade.

Trade changes settlements.

Religion changes politics.

Politics affects ownership.

Ownership affects rebellion.

Rifts affect resources.

Resources affect technology.

Soulbeasts affect warfare.

And NPC relationships affect all of it.

That interconnected simulation is what can make the world feel genuinely alive rather than merely large.
Yes. The **war-map + live-battle combination is much stronger than choosing only one**. The strategic layer gives you the feeling of actually commanding territory and armies, while live battles keep the game personal. The important part is making them the **same war simulation**, not two disconnected game modes.

Also, the animation packs are a foundation, not automatically “perfect.” For Rising Ashes, every important animation still needs retargeting, root-motion checks, foot-locking, transition blending, stance consistency, hit timing and mobile-performance testing. Your elemental martial arts should eventually modify those animations rather than just attaching VFX to generic attacks.

I’ve also kept the new settlement/army/war direction as part of the ongoing Rising Ashes design.
